Part 1 3D Imaging — How to Read MRI Data

▶

From raw scanner output to a navigable digital model of the living brain. MRI data aren't mysterious black boxes — with the right tools, you can open, inspect, and convert them yourself.

Step 1 — Obtain MRI Data▶

Publicly available brain templates and reference datasets are the foundation of neuroimaging research. These standard atlases provide a common coordinate system for comparing brains across studies and individuals.

  • MNI152 — the most widely used standard brain template, averaged from 152 healthy adults
  • Colin27 — a high-resolution single-subject template, scanned 27 times and averaged for exceptional detail
  • ICBM152 — the International Consortium for Brain Mapping probabilistic atlas
Open NIST Atlases at MNI →
Step 2 — How to View MRI Files▶

MRI data typically come in two formats: DICOM (raw scanner output, one file per slice) and NIfTI (a single compressed 3D/4D file, more convenient for analysis). You'll need a viewer to open them:

3D Slicer
Full-featured platform for visualization, segmentation, and 3D model export. Supports DICOM, NIfTI, and dozens of other formats. Cross-platform, free, and open-source.
slicer.org →
MRIcron / MRIcroGL
Lightweight, fast NIfTI viewer by Chris Rorden. Excellent for quick inspection and creating figures for publications. MRIcroGL adds 3D rendering.
nitrc.org →
FSLeyes
Part of the FSL suite. Clean Python-based viewer with excellent overlay support — view multiple modalities (T1, fMRI, DTI) simultaneously.
fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk →
ITK-SNAP
Specialized in semi-automatic segmentation. Great for tracing regions of interest and exporting labeled volumes.
itksnap.org →
Step 3 — Convert MRI Data Into a 3D Brain Model▶

Turning an MRI scan into a 3D-printable brain model typically follows this pipeline:

DICOM → NIfTI → Segmentation (FreeSurfer) → Surface Extraction → STL Export → Mesh Cleanup → Print

Key tools for this pipeline:

  • FreeSurfer — automated cortical and subcortical segmentation from T1 MRI (Linux/macOS, ~10–24 hrs per subject). The gold standard for surface reconstruction.
  • 3D Slicer — DICOM import, manual segmentation, STL export. The easiest entry point for beginners.
  • MeshLab — mesh repair, smoothing, decimation, and hemisphere merging. Free, cross-platform.
  • dcm2niix — converts DICOM to NIfTI format. Indispensable utility.

📚 Essential reading: Andy's Brain Book — the best open-access introduction to MRI data analysis and neuroimaging workflows. Covers everything from installing tools to running full analyses with clear, step-by-step tutorials.
Bridge question: Now that you've seen the data — do you actually understand what you're looking at? Why do those English abbreviations in neuroimaging papers still make us tense up?

The gap isn't the technology. It's that most of us have never built a direct, intuitive, three-dimensional mental map of brain anatomy. We learn brain regions as lists of names — not as spatial neighbors you can rotate, peel apart, and walk through. That's what the next section is for.

Part 2 3D Brain Anatomy — Understanding the Brain Through 3D Technology

▶

Building an intuitive, three-dimensional mental model of brain architecture — so you can navigate neuroimaging papers and data with confidence.

Step 1 — Decoding the Brain Region Puzzle▶

Brain regions have multiple naming conventions — Brodmann areas, anatomical landmarks, functional designations — and they don't always align neatly. The key is to stop memorizing and start navigating.

Common atlases used in neuroimaging:

  • AAL (Automated Anatomical Labeling) — 116 regions, the most widely cited parcellation in fMRI literature
  • Harvard-Oxford Atlas — probabilistic atlas distributed with FSL, excellent for structural connectivity
  • Desikan-Killiany — 68 cortical regions, the default output of FreeSurfer's recon-all
  • HCP-MMP1.0 — 180 areas per hemisphere from the Human Connectome Project, multi-modal parcellation

Rather than memorizing these, the goal is to build spatial intuition — reach the point where "superior temporal sulcus" or "anterior cingulate" evokes a clear three-dimensional image in your mind. That's what Step 2 is about.

Step 2 — Constructing a 3D Understanding of Brain Regions▶

Interactive 3D brain atlases are the fastest way to build spatial intuition. Instead of looking at flat textbook slices, you can rotate, zoom, and peel apart brain structures in real time.

3D Brain (Cold Spring Harbor Lab)
The tool we use in this workshop. 29 interactive structures, clean interface, runs in a browser. Ideal for first-time explorers.
Open 3D Brain →
BrainFacts.org 3D Brain
Similar interactive atlas with detailed descriptions of each structure's function and associated disorders.
Open BrainFacts →
The Human Brain Atlas (MSU)
High-resolution stained sections with MRI correlation. Excellent for understanding the cytoarchitectonic basis of MRI-visible structures.
Open Atlas →
Neuroglancer (Google)
WebGL-based viewer for petabyte-scale 3D brain data. Can browse electron microscopy volumes at synaptic resolution.
Try Demo →

Practice tip: pick a brain region mentioned in a paper you're reading. Find it in 3D Brain. Rotate it. Look at its neighbors. Ask yourself: if this region were damaged, what would change? This habit transforms passive reading into active spatial learning.

Part 3 3D Modeling & 3D Printing — Turning Imagination Into Reality

▶

From digital models to physical objects you can hold in your hands. What can 3D printing actually do for psychology — and how do you start?

Step 1 — What Can 3D Printing Do in Psychology Research?▶

3D printing is already transforming psychology and neuroscience — from tangible teaching tools to personalized therapeutic devices. A growing body of research supports its effectiveness:

🧠 Neuroanatomy Education

3D-printed brain models consistently improve spatial understanding compared to textbook-only instruction. Studies across multiple institutions show gains in accuracy, satisfaction, and engagement. Learners who physically manipulate models outperform screen-only peers. (Aridan et al., 2024; Minear et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2026)

🏃 Youth Behavior Change & Identity

3D-printed physical activity models gave adolescents a tangible representation of their exercise data — displayed proudly on desks, compared with peers, and used as conversation starters. The physical artifact became a locus of identity and reflection. (IJERPH, 2023)

Read paper →

🩺 Prenatal Attachment & Mental Health

An RCT (n=184) found that 3D-printed fetal models and 3D pictures both significantly improved prenatal attachment and reduced depression and anxiety in expectant parents. The tangible representation of the fetus had measurable psychological benefits. (JOGNN, 2023)

Read paper →

🧓 Reminiscence Therapy for Older Adults

3D-printed objects from older adults' personal histories — vintage cars, bicycles, household items, pets — served as powerful multisensory memory cues, adding tactile depth beyond photographs and facilitating relationship-building between participants and researchers. (J. Applied Gerontology, 2024)

Read paper →

🤝 Inclusive Education & Peer Interaction

3D printing as a cooperative learning tool improved peer communication, knowledge retention, and social dynamics between students with and without disabilities. Students reported preferring future 3D-printing-based lessons. (Intl. J. Disability, Dev. & Education, 2024)

🦾 Pediatric Prosthetics & Well-Being

Custom 3D-printed myoelectric prostheses with personalized, child-friendly theming significantly improved psychological well-being and quality of life in pediatric amputees. The aesthetic personalization was as important as the mechanical function. (2024)

Step 2 — 3D Modeling Platforms▶

Choose a platform to create or modify your 3D model. We strongly recommend Tinkercad — it runs entirely in your browser, requires zero installation, and has the gentlest learning curve. It's what most first-time modelers use successfully.

★ Tinkercad (Recommended)

Other platforms — more powerful but steeper learning curves:

MakerWorld FreeCAD OpenSCAD Hunyuan3D

💡 Tinkercad vs. the others: Tinkercad is a direct-manipulation modeler (drag, resize, group). FreeCAD and OpenSCAD are parametric modelers (define dimensions and constraints). Hunyuan3D uses AI to generate models from text or images. Start with Tinkercad, explore the others as your needs grow.

Step 3 — 3D Printing▶

Once your digital model is ready, you need two things: a printer and slicing software that prepares your model for printing.

📥 JGAurora A6 Printer Manual (PDF)

The JGAurora A6 is the 3D printer available at the Department of Psychology, Zhejiang University. If you're using a different printer, please refer to your own device's documentation — the core concepts are similar, but specifics vary by model.

Ultimaker Cura — Slicing Software →

Cura is the most widely used free slicing software. It converts your 3D model (STL/OBJ) into printer instructions (G-code), letting you adjust layer height, infill density, supports, and print speed. Works with most FDM printers, not just Ultimaker machines.
