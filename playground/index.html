Part 1 3D Imaging — From MRI Principles to Data Processing

▶

Understanding the basic principles and appropriate use of functional MRI is the first step in designing a brain imaging study. This section covers the full workflow: deciding whether to use fMRI, scheduling a scanner, acquiring data, and processing it.

Step 1 — MRI Fundamentals & Study Preparation▶

How fMRI works

fMRI measures the BOLD (Blood Oxygenation Level Dependent) signal — an indirect, vascular proxy for neural activity. When neurons fire, local blood flow increases, altering the ratio of oxygenated to deoxygenated hemoglobin. Deoxyhemoglobin is paramagnetic and reduces the MR signal; its washout causes the signal to rise. The BOLD response peaks roughly 5–10 seconds after neural activity, lagging by 1–2 seconds. fMRI therefore measures a hemodynamic response coupled to neural activity, not neural activity itself.

When to use fMRI

  • Task-based fMRI: localizing cognitive functions to brain regions; testing hypotheses that predict specific regional activation; comparing brain activity across experimental conditions.
  • Resting-state fMRI: identifying large-scale functional brain networks; studying populations unable to perform tasks (infants, Alzheimer's patients, etc.); large-scale population neuroscience.

When NOT to use fMRI

  • Questions requiring causal inference (fMRI is correlational)
  • Millisecond-timescale neural dynamics (use EEG/MEG)
  • Single-subject clinical diagnosis (except pre-surgical mapping)
  • Constructs that cannot be effectively operationalized inside a scanner
  • Individual-difference studies with small samples (univariate methods may require N > 1,000)

Key limitations

  • Head motion artifacts, especially problematic in developmental, psychiatric, and pediatric populations
  • High within- and between-subject variability in the BOLD signal
  • Analytic flexibility — one systematic evaluation tested 768 fMRI pipelines and most failed to produce consistent results
  • Blood pressure, heart rate, caffeine, diet, and circadian phase all affect the BOLD signal

On the day of scanning

  • Preparation: confirm ethics approval; prepare consent forms; run behavioral pilot testing to optimize task design before scanning
  • Participant reception: explain the procedure, scan duration, and requirements clearly; ensure informed consent is signed
  • Safety screening (critical): verify no metal implants (pacemakers, aneurysm clips, metal dental work, orthopedic hardware); remove all metal items (glasses, hairpins, jewelry, watches, belts, clothing with metal accessories); confirm no tattoos (some tattoo pigments contain metal)
  • Scanning: performed by the MRI technician — researchers do not need to operate the scanner
  • Data retrieval: collect DICOM-format raw data from the technician after scanning

From Part 1 to Part 2
Acquiring and viewing MRI data clears a technical hurdle, but it does not confer anatomical understanding on its own. The limiting factor is rarely the data or the software — it is the absence of a robust three-dimensional spatial understanding of brain anatomy. The next section addresses this gap.

Part 2 3D Brain Anatomy — Building Spatial Understanding

▶

Neuroimaging papers routinely reference brain regions by anatomical or functional labels. Navigating this literature requires more than name recognition — it demands a spatial representation of where structures sit relative to one another in three dimensions.

Step 1 — How Brain Regions Are Defined & Labeled▶

Multiple parcellation schemes exist, each dividing the brain according to different principles: cytoarchitecture (Brodmann areas), gross anatomy (gyral and sulcal landmarks), or functional connectivity. These schemes do not always align — the same anatomical location can carry different labels across atlases.

Commonly used parcellation atlases:

  • AAL — 116 regions (90 cortical, 26 cerebellar). The most frequently cited parcellation in the fMRI literature.
  • Harvard-Oxford — a probabilistic atlas distributed with FSL. Each voxel carries a probability of belonging to a given structure.
  • Desikan-Killiany — 68 cortical regions (34 per hemisphere). The default FreeSurfer recon-all output.
  • HCP-MMP 1.0 — 180 areas per hemisphere, derived from multi-modal Human Connectome Project data.
Step 2 — Interactive Tools for Learning 3D Brain Anatomy▶

Traditional neuroanatomy instruction relies on two-dimensional representations. These interactive atlases allow direct exploration through rotation, depth peeling, and selective highlighting:

From Part 2 to Part 3
Three-dimensional atlases build spatial knowledge, but knowledge remains abstract until tested through production. When a researcher segments a brain region from data and converts it into a physical object — checking whether the printed model matches anatomical expectation — learning becomes self-correcting. The transition from digital exploration to physical fabrication closes a feedback loop. The next section introduces the tools for this transition.

Part 3 3D Modeling & Printing — From Digital Model to Physical Object

▶

The digital brain models generated in Part 1, now anatomically understood through Part 2, can be materialized through 3D printing. This section surveys research applications and provides practical tools to get started.

Step 1 — Research Applications of 3D Printing▶

3D printing has been applied across several research domains. Representative findings with accessible sources:

Prenatal Attachment

An RCT (n = 184) found that 3D-printed fetal models and 3D pictures both significantly improved prenatal attachment scores and reduced depression and anxiety in expectant parents.

Coté et al., 2023, JOGNN

Reminiscence Therapy

3D-printed replicas of personally meaningful objects served as effective multisensory memory cues in reminiscence therapy with older adults, providing retrieval advantages over photographs alone.

Anderson et al., 2024, J. Applied Gerontology
Step 2 — 3D Modeling Software▶

Tinkercad runs in your browser, uses direct manipulation (drag, resize, group), and requires no prior experience — recommended as the starting point. Other tools, each with distinct strengths:

Tinkercad (Recommended) Blender MeshLab Fusion 360 FreeCAD OpenSCAD MeshMixer Hunyuan3D

Blender: professional open-source 3D suite (modeling, sculpting, rendering). MeshLab: mesh processing — repair, smoothing, decimation. MeshMixer: print preparation — hollowing and support generation. Fusion 360: professional CAD/CAM, free for education. FreeCAD / OpenSCAD: parametric modeling. Hunyuan3D: AI-generated 3D models from text or images.

Step 3 — 3D Printing Workflow▶

Printing requires two components: the printer hardware and slicing software that translates your model into machine instructions.

JGAurora A6 Printer Manual (PDF)

The JGAurora A6 is the FDM printer available at the Department of Psychology, ZJU. Users with other printer models should consult their device's documentation; the general workflow described here applies to all FDM printers.

Ultimaker Cura — Slicing Software

Cura converts 3D models (STL, OBJ, 3MF) into G-code — the instructions that control printer movement, temperature, and extrusion. Compatible with most FDM printers.

From Part 3 to Part 4
3D printing brings anatomical models into physical space. But 3D technology also works in the reverse direction: immersing a participant within a fully synthetic environment. Virtual reality extends the logic of the previous sections — spatial understanding, digital modeling, real-time interaction — into the domain of experimental research, where environmental variables can be controlled with precision unattainable in physical laboratories.

Part 4 Experiments in 3D Space — A Practical Introduction to VR

▶

Virtual reality (VR) head-mounted displays enable the construction of fully controlled three-dimensional experimental environments. This section provides a practical guide, from deciding whether VR is the right tool to building and running your first VR experiment.

Step 1 — Why VR in Research▶

Key advantages: real-time head tracking, binocular disparity for genuine depth perception, the ability to construct environments that do not exist in reality, and safe presentation of hazardous scenarios. When these benefits outweigh the limitations, VR is the appropriate method.

Main limitations: cybersickness; headset weight and comfort; distance compression (perceived distances are systematically shorter than physical distances); incompatibility with fMRI scanners.

Step 2 — Setting Up Unity for VR Development▶

After completing these steps, you will have a working VR scene you can test immediately.

1. Install Unity via Unity Hub (2022 LTS or later). 2. Create a project using the 3D Core template. 3. Configure OpenXR: Edit → Project Settings → XR Plugin Management → Install and enable OpenXR → click Fix All. 4. Add controller profiles under Interaction Profiles (HTC Vive / Oculus Touch). 5. Install XR Interaction Toolkit: Package Manager → add com.unity.xr.interaction.toolkit → import Starter Assets. 6. Create XR Origin: Hierarchy → XR → XR Origin (Action-based) → drag Starter Assets controller presets onto the Inspector. 7. Test interaction: create a Cube → add XR Grab Interactable → Play → press Grip to grab.

XRI's interaction architecture has three roles: Interactor (the initiator — controller rays and hand colliders), Interactable (the object being interacted with), and Interaction Manager (coordinates all pairs).

Step 3 — VR Development Workflow▶

Once the environment is set up, a full VR program goes through four stages:

1. Build the scene — model from scratch (Blender, etc.) or use existing assets (Unity Asset Store, Sketchfab). Start with greyboxing: use simple geometric stand-ins to validate layout and interaction before importing detailed models.

2. Add interaction logic — attach scripts to objects to define how controller input maps to object behavior. Three basic patterns: ray interaction (distant pointing and selection), grab interaction (nearby pickup and manipulation), and collision detection (area-based triggers).

3. Connect the hardware — power on the headset and controllers → launch SteamVR → ensure Unity uses OpenXR → place XR Origin in the scene → press Play to preview in the headset.

4. Build and deploy — PC VR exports as .exe (or run directly in the Unity Editor). Standalone headsets (Meta Quest, Pico) build to APK for sideloading.

Step 7 — Major Research Applications▶

Spatial Navigation & Episodic Memory

VR has become a standard method for studying human spatial navigation, with experimenters controlling every landmark and spatial cue precisely. This methodology played an important role in the discovery of grid-cell-like representations in humans (Doeller et al., 2010, Nature).

Social Cognition

VR enables systematic manipulation of social cues — gaze direction, interpersonal distance, facial expressions, and avatar appearance — while tightly controlling confounds. Applied in social anxiety research and implicit bias studies.

Exposure Therapy

VR exposure therapy is among the most mature clinical applications of immersive technology. RCT evidence supports its efficacy for acrophobia, flight phobia, spider phobia, PTSD, and social anxiety. The key advantage is precise dose control.

Risk Decision-Making

VR can present genuinely hazardous scenarios (fire evacuation, traffic accidents) in a zero-risk environment — enabling the study of emergency decision processes that cannot be examined in reality for obvious ethical reasons.
