How 3D Technology Supports Psychology Research

Part 1 3D Imaging — Reading and Interpreting MRI Data

▶

Structural MRI provides a three-dimensional representation of the living brain at millimeter resolution. This section introduces the standard data formats, viewing tools, and processing pipelines that make neuroimaging data accessible to researchers without a radiology background.

Step 1 — Obtain Standard Brain Templates▶

Standard brain templates provide a common coordinate system for comparing brains across studies and individuals. These atlases are the reference frame of nearly all neuroimaging research.

  • MNI152 — the most widely used standard-space template, averaged from T1-weighted scans of 152 healthy young adults. Defines the coordinate system (MNI space) used in the majority of published fMRI studies.
  • Colin27 — a high-resolution single-subject template produced by scanning one individual 27 times and averaging the acquisitions. Provides finer anatomical detail than MNI152.
  • ICBM152 — a probabilistic atlas developed by the International Consortium for Brain Mapping, combining T1, T2, and proton-density contrasts.

Step 2 — View and Inspect MRI Data▶

MRI data are stored in two primary formats: DICOM (the raw output from the scanner, one file per slice) and NIfTI (a single file containing the full 3D or 4D volume, widely used in analysis). Several free, open-source viewers are available:

3D Slicer
Comprehensive platform for visualization, manual and semi-automatic segmentation, and 3D model export. Supports DICOM, NIfTI, and numerous other formats.
slicer.org
MRIcroGL
Lightweight viewer with GPU-accelerated 3D rendering. Rapid loading of NIfTI files; suitable for quick inspection and publication-quality screenshots.
nitrc.org
FSLeyes
Part of the FSL analysis suite. Supports simultaneous overlay of multiple modalities (T1, fMRI statistical maps, DTI) with linked cursor navigation.
fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk
ITK-SNAP
Designed for semi-automatic segmentation using active contour methods. Useful for delineating regions of interest and exporting labeled volumetric data.
itksnap.org
Step 3 — From MRI Volume to 3D Surface Model▶

The standard pipeline for converting a structural T1-weighted MRI scan into a 3D-printable brain model proceeds through the following stages:

DICOM → NIfTI (dcm2niix) → Cortical Segmentation (FreeSurfer recon-all) → Surface Extraction (pial surface) → Mesh Conversion (STL) → Mesh Cleanup (MeshLab/Blender) → 3D Print

Primary tools in this pipeline:

  • FreeSurfer — performs automated volumetric segmentation and cortical surface reconstruction from T1-weighted images. The standard tool for extracting the pial surface and gray/white boundary. Computationally intensive (~10–24 hours per subject).
  • 3D Slicer — provides a more accessible entry point for manual or semi-automatic segmentation and direct STL export without command-line operation.
  • MeshLab — open-source mesh processing tool for repair, smoothing, decimation, and merging of surface files before printing.
  • dcm2niix — converts DICOM series to NIfTI format. An essential preprocessing utility included with MRIcron.
Complete Pipeline Guide (Burles, 2024) Columbia Quick-Start Guide (PDF)
Recommended reference: Andy's Brain Book — a comprehensive open-access introduction to MRI data analysis, covering tool installation, preprocessing, and statistical analysis with step-by-step tutorials.
From Part 1 to Part 2
Acquiring and viewing MRI data is a technical achievement, but it does not by itself confer anatomical understanding. A researcher can load a NIfTI volume in 3D Slicer and navigate to any coordinate in MNI space — yet still lack the spatial intuition to interpret what a given voxel represents. The limiting factor is typically not access to data or software; it is the absence of a robust three-dimensional mental model of brain anatomy. The following section addresses this gap.

Part 2 3D Brain Anatomy — Building Spatial Understanding

▶

Neuroimaging papers routinely reference brain regions by anatomical or functional labels. Navigating this literature requires more than name recognition — it demands a spatial representation of where structures sit relative to one another in three dimensions.

Step 1 — How Brain Regions Are Defined and Labeled▶

Multiple parcellation schemes exist, each dividing the brain into regions according to different principles: cytoarchitecture (Brodmann areas), gross anatomy (gyral and sulcal landmarks), or functional connectivity. These schemes do not always align, and a single anatomical location may carry several labels across different atlases.

Major parcellation atlases used in neuroimaging:

  • AAL (Automated Anatomical Labeling) — 116 regions (90 cortical, 26 cerebellar). The most frequently cited parcellation in fMRI literature, used for region-of-interest analyses and meta-analytic labeling.
  • Harvard-Oxford Atlas — a probabilistic atlas distributed with FSL. Each voxel carries a probability of belonging to a given structure, reflecting inter-subject variability.
  • Desikan-Killiany — 68 cortical regions (34 per hemisphere). The default cortical parcellation output by FreeSurfer's recon-all pipeline.
  • HCP-MMP 1.0 — 180 areas per hemisphere, derived from multi-modal data (cortical architecture, function, connectivity, and topography) in the Human Connectome Project. A more recent and finer-grained alternative.
Step 2 — Interactive Tools for Learning Brain Anatomy in 3D▶

Traditional neuroanatomy instruction relies on two-dimensional representations — textbook plates, stained sections, or orthogonal MRI views. These are informative but do not convey the spatial relationships that become immediately apparent when the brain is rendered as a manipulable three-dimensional object. The following interactive atlases allow direct exploration of brain structures through rotation, depth peeling, and selective highlighting:

3D Brain (Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory)
29 labeled interactive structures with concise functional summaries. Browser-based; no installation required. The primary reference tool used in this workshop.
g2conline.org/3dbrain
BrainFacts.org 3D Brain
Interactive atlas with detailed descriptions of each structure's function and associated clinical conditions.
brainfacts.org/3d-brain
MSU Human Brain Atlas
High-resolution histological sections correlated with MRI. Useful for understanding the cytoarchitectonic basis of signal variation in structural images.
brains.anatomy.msu.edu
Neuroglancer
WebGL-based viewer capable of rendering petabyte-scale volumetric data. Supports browsing of electron microscopy volumes at synaptic resolution alongside MRI data.
neuroglancer-demo.appspot.com
From Part 2 to Part 3
Three-dimensional atlases build spatial knowledge, but knowledge remains abstract until it is tested through production. When a researcher segments a brain region and converts it into a physical object — checking whether the printed model matches anatomical expectation — the learning becomes self-correcting. The transition from digital exploration to physical fabrication is not merely additive; it closes a feedback loop in which spatial understanding is continuously refined through the act of making. The following section introduces the tools and methods for this transition.

Part 3 3D Modeling & Printing — From Digital Model to Physical Object

▶

The digital brain models produced in Part 1, now anatomically understood through Part 2, can be materialized through 3D printing. This section surveys the research applications of 3D printing in psychology and provides the practical tools to begin.

Step 1 — Research Applications of 3D Printing in Psychology▶

3D printing has been applied across several domains of psychological research. Representative findings are summarized below.

Neuroanatomy Education

Multiple controlled studies demonstrate that students who physically manipulate 3D-printed brain models achieve superior spatial understanding compared to those using textbooks or screen-based 3D renderings alone. The tactile component appears to confer a distinct learning advantage.

Aridan et al., 2024; Minear et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2026

Prenatal Attachment

A randomized controlled trial (n = 184) found that 3D-printed fetal models significantly improved maternal and paternal prenatal attachment scores and reduced depression and pregnancy-related anxiety. The effect was observed for both 3D-printed models and 3D-printed pictures.

JOGNN, 2023

Youth Physical Activity Behavior

Adolescents given 3D-printed models representing their personal physical activity data displayed the models as identity markers, compared them with peers, and reported increased self-reflection on exercise habits. The physical artifact served as a locus of behavioral identity.

IJERPH, 2023

Reminiscence Therapy

3D-printed replicas of personally meaningful objects served as effective memory cues in reminiscence therapy with older adults. Multisensory engagement provided retrieval advantages beyond those observed with photographs alone.

J. Applied Gerontology, 2024

Inclusive Classroom Interaction

3D printing used as a cooperative learning tool improved peer communication and knowledge retention among students with and without disabilities. Students expressed a preference for 3D-printing-based lessons over traditional formats.

Intl. J. Disability, Dev. & Education, 2024

Pediatric Prosthetics

Customized 3D-printed myoelectric prostheses with personalized aesthetic design improved psychological well-being in pediatric amputees. Personalization of the device's appearance was rated as important as its mechanical function.

Lecture Notes in Networks and Systems, 2024
Step 2 — 3D Modeling Software▶

Creating or editing a 3D model requires modeling software. Tinkercad is recommended as the starting point: it operates entirely within a web browser, requires no prior experience, and uses direct manipulation (drag, resize, group) rather than parametric definition. This makes it accessible to first-time users while still capable of producing models suitable for printing.

Tinkercad (Recommended)

Additional platforms, each suited to different modeling paradigms:

MakerWorld FreeCAD OpenSCAD Hunyuan3D

FreeCAD and OpenSCAD are parametric modelers (geometry defined by constraints and scripts). Hunyuan3D generates 3D models from text descriptions or images using deep learning. MakerWorld is a community platform for sharing and remixing printable models. For most workshop participants, Tinkercad will be the most direct path from concept to printable file.

Step 3 — 3D Printing Workflow▶

Printing a 3D model requires two components: the printer hardware and slicing software that translates the model into machine instructions.

JGAurora A6 Printer Manual (PDF)

The JGAurora A6 is the fused deposition modeling (FDM) printer available at the Department of Psychology, Zhejiang University. This document describes its operation and specifications. Users working with other printer models should consult the documentation for their specific device; the general workflow described here — model preparation, slicing, printing — is common to all FDM printers.

Ultimaker Cura — Slicing Software

Cura converts a 3D model (STL, OBJ, or 3MF format) into G-code — the low-level set of instructions that controls the printer's movements, temperatures, and extrusion. It provides control over layer height, infill density, support structures, and print speed. Despite its name, Cura is compatible with most FDM printers, not only Ultimaker machines.

From Part 3 to Part 4
Three-dimensional printing brings anatomical models into physical space, where they can be handled, annotated, and shared. But 3D technology also operates in the reverse direction: rather than materializing digital objects, it can immerse a participant within a fully synthetic environment. Virtual and augmented reality extend the logic of the previous sections — spatial understanding, digital modeling, real-time interaction — into the domain of experimental psychology, where environmental variables can be controlled with a precision unattainable in physical laboratories.

Part 4 Experiments in 3D Space — Virtual & Augmented Reality

▶

Virtual reality (VR) enables the construction of fully controlled three-dimensional experimental environments. Augmented reality (AR) overlays digital information onto the physical world. Both technologies extend the principles introduced in the previous sections — spatial representation, digital modeling, and real-time 3D interaction — into the domain of psychological experimentation.

Spatial Navigation and Episodic Memory▶

VR has become the standard method for studying human spatial navigation. Participants can be placed in large-scale virtual environments — city blocks, radial mazes, natural landscapes — while their path, gaze, and physiological responses are recorded with millisecond precision. The experimenter controls every landmark, the geometry of the space, and the availability of distal cues, achieving a level of experimental control that is impossible in real-world navigation studies. This methodology has been central to the study of grid-cell-like representations in humans (Doeller et al., 2010) and to identifying age-related changes in navigational strategies.

Social Cognition in Immersive Environments▶

VR enables the systematic manipulation of social cues — gaze direction, interpersonal distance, facial expression, and avatar appearance — while maintaining experimental confounds to a minimum. This has been applied to the study of social anxiety, where participants interact with virtual audiences or conversation partners whose behavior can be parametrically varied. It has also been used to investigate phenomena such as personal space regulation and the perceptual processes underlying implicit racial bias, where the experimenter can independently manipulate skin tone, facial morphology, and behavioral mannerisms.

Exposure Therapy and Clinical Applications▶

VR-based exposure therapy is among the most extensively validated clinical applications of immersive technology. Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated its efficacy for specific phobias (fear of heights, flying, spiders), post-traumatic stress disorder, and social anxiety disorder. The key advantage is dose control: the therapist can titrate the intensity of the feared stimulus with a precision unattainable in vivo, and the session can be terminated immediately if distress exceeds a manageable threshold. Meta-analyses indicate effect sizes comparable to in-vivo exposure, with lower dropout rates in some populations.

AR for Behavioral Annotation and Augmented Perception▶

Augmented reality presents distinct experimental possibilities from VR. Because AR overlays digital content onto the participant's actual environment, it is suited for studies in which ecological validity is paramount: real-time annotation of social interactions, augmented perceptual cues superimposed on physical objects, and context-aware prompting in naturalistic settings. Current research is exploring AR-based interventions for visuospatial neglect and for Parkinson's disease, where visual cues projected into the patient's field of view can modulate gait parameters in real time.
